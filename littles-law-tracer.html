01What This Tool Measures

Little's Law says lead time equals the work in the system divided by the rate work leaves it. In a stable system, it always holds. This tool compares the lead time the math predicts with the lead time you actually measure. The gap between the two is hidden delay.

02Where This Comes From

John Little, an operations researcher at MIT, proved the law in 1961. People had used it informally before that. Little showed it holds for almost any stable system: a coffee line, a hospital ward, a production line, a sales pipeline.

Because it is proven, it doesn't fail. It tells the truth about your numbers whether you like it or not. If your measured lead time runs well past the prediction, something the math can't see is adding time. Blocked work, multitasking, and queues nobody counts are the usual causes.

03Why It Matters

Most teams try to go faster by pushing people harder. Little's Law points at a different lever: how much work is open. Cut WIP, keep the exit rate the same, and lead time drops. The math requires it. This is the logic behind WIP limits.

04How To Use It

  • WIP: every item started and not finished. Count the ones that are blocked or waiting.
  • Exit Rate: how many items you actually finish in a typical week. Not the target.
  • Actual Lead Time: from real dates, the day an item entered to the day it left. Not an estimate.
  • Days per week: 7 if your lead times count calendar days, 5 if they count working days. Match how your dates are counted.

05Reading Your Results

Clean Flow (0 to 15%): actual lead time is close to the prediction. Work moves as fast as your WIP and exit rate allow.

Some Delay (16 to 40%): a modest gap. Worth watching, not urgent.

Heavy Delay (41 to 80%): work takes much longer than the math says it should. Look for blocked work, task switching, or WIP that isn't being counted.

Broken Flow (81% and up): something structural is wrong. Work is likely sitting idle for most of its time in the system.

06Reading The Chart

Gold bars show the predicted lead time. Navy bars show what you measured. Under each workstream name is the math behind the prediction. When the navy bar runs past the gold one, the extra length is hidden delay. Go look at it directly.

07Common Traps

Hidden WIP. Work sitting in an inbox, an approval queue, or someone's private list still counts. Leave it out and the prediction looks better than reality.

Mismatched days. If your lead times skip weekends but the setting says 7, every workstream looks about 40% better than it is.

More coming in than going out. The law assumes a stable system. If work enters faster than it leaves, WIP keeps growing and today's prediction will be out of date by next week.

08Connects To

Heavy or Broken Flow is Norman's Gap you can measure: pressure to take on more work outrunning the regulation that controls WIP. If a workstream keeps showing hidden delay, check whether work enters faster than it leaves. Then set a hard WIP limit with the WIP Limit Finder.

09Field Note

Nobody sets out to bury their team under open work. It happens one reasonable yes at a time, until the math catches up. Little's Law doesn't care how it happened. It tells you exactly where you stand.
