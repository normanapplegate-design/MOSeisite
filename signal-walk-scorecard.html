What the Analysis tab tells you

Each card in Analysis answers one plain question about a meeting. This page gives the question, an example, and what good looks like. No math needed.

Start here: chances and misses

Every number comes from counting two things on each walk. A chance is one thing the meeting should get right: started on time, board updated, Materials in the room. A miss is a chance it got wrong.

Example: a Tier 2 walk has 24 things to check. The meeting gets 22 right and 2 wrong. That walk has 24 chances and 2 misses. Everything else is these two counts, added up across walks and looked at from different angles.

A walk is at standard when its score lands at Regulating or better on the IMI scale. Think of it as a passing grade.

The IMI scale

What do Dark, Compliant, and Regulating mean?

IMI stands for Implementation Maturity Index. It is a seven-step ladder from "the meeting does not really exist" to "the meeting runs the business." Every walk lands on one step.

The step that matters most is the jump from Compliant to Regulating. A Compliant meeting goes through the motions: the board is walked, the boxes get ticked. A Regulating meeting changes what people do afterward. That is why Regulating is the passing line.

Example: a meeting that starts on time, walks the board, and assigns owners, but never escalates anything and lets the action log go stale, usually lands at Compliant. Fix the escalations and the log, and it moves to Regulating.

Sigma level: how often does it miss?

Out of all the chances, how many did the meeting miss?

Miss rate, DPMO, and sigma all answer that one question in different units, like miles, kilometers, and feet. 2 misses in 24 chances is about an 8% miss rate. Per million, that is 80,000 DPMO. As a sigma score, where higher is better, it is about 2.9.

Each step up in sigma is harder than the last. Cutting misses from 30% to 15% gains half a point. Cutting from 7% to 5% gains a tenth. The last few misses are always the hardest to remove.

Zero-miss walks are rare by design. With 24 chances to slip, even a good meeting comes out perfect about 3 walks in 10.

Good: miss rate 5% or lower, about 3.1 sigma.

Control chart: normal, or did something change?

One walk had 20% misses and the next had 5%. Real change, or the normal up and down?

Every meeting wobbles, the way a commute takes 22 minutes one day and 26 the next. The chart draws a band around the normal wobble. A walk inside the band is noise. A walk outside it means something specific happened that day, and it is worth asking what.

Three warning signs, shown in red or written under the chart: a dot outside the band, 8 dots in a row on one side of the middle line, or 6 dots in a row heading the same way.

Steady is not the same as good. A meeting can be steady at 30% misses. The band settles after about 20 walks.

Good: no warning signs for 20 walks, middle line at 5% or lower.

Sustainment: is it holding?

It looked good last month. Is it still good, and will it be good tomorrow?

Chance the next walk passes: if 8 of the last 10 passed, about 75%. Walks in a row at standard: like a safety board's days without an incident. Trend: misses going up, down, or flat. It only says improving or decaying when the change is clear.

Good: 80% chance or higher, 10 or more in a row, trend improving, or flat once at target.

Learning curve: when will it get there?

The team improves with practice. At this pace, when does the meeting hit target?

Like learning to drive: big gains in the first 10 trips, small ones by trip 500. Every time the team doubles the meetings it has held, misses drop by about the same share. If 25 meetings in it misses 20%, and at 50 meetings it misses 16%, each doubling kept 80% of the misses. That is a learning rate of 80%. Lower is faster.

"More than 3 years out" means practice alone will not close the gap. Change something, such as coaching, the agenda, or a standard, and watch the curve bend. Enter the meeting's real start date and how often it meets. After a chair change, set a new start date.

Good: learning rate 85% or lower, target date inside the engagement.

Pareto: where are the misses coming from?

Which checks get missed most, so we know what to coach first?

A few checks usually cause most of the trouble. 9/26 means missed 9 times out of the 26 times the check applied. If the top 3 carry half the misses, coaching those 3 habits cuts misses in half. Trust 9/26 more than 3/4: four times is not much to go on.

Good: no check missed more than 1 time in 10.

Before and after: did the coaching work?

We coached the chair on the 13th. Better meeting, or a good week?

Pick the date. The tool compares the walks before and after, and runs two separate checks. Real change means both agree. Leaning better means one agrees, so keep walking. Could be noise means neither does. About 10 walks on each side gives a trustworthy answer.

Good: Real change, in the better direction, after each round of coaching.

Results: do better meetings bring better numbers?

When the meeting runs well, does the area hit its targets more often?

Example: Delivery hit chance 35% at a 50% meeting score, 57% at a 90% score. A well-run meeting nearly doubles the odds. An asterisk means the link is strong enough to trust. The next walk table carries more weight than same walk, because the meeting happens first. A strong link still does not prove cause: a good supervisor may run better meetings and get better results.

Good: at least one result with an asterisk on the next walk table.

Participation: does the room own the meeting?

Do the people in the room speak to their own numbers, or does the chair do all the talking?

Example: 9 people in the room, 5 spoke besides the chair. That is 5 of 8, about 63%.

Good: half the room or more.

Decision Boundary: is the room making its own calls?

Does this team decide what it is allowed to decide?

Hesitations count each "I'll have to check" about something the room could have decided. Escalations sort into boundary (truly needed someone higher), clock (could not be fixed in time here), or just hard (the team owned it and passed it up anyway). A high just hard share means the meeting reports status instead of making decisions.

Good: hesitations heading to zero, just hard at 1 in 10 or fewer.

Kappa: do two observers see the same meeting?

If you and a colleague score the same meeting, do you get the same answers?

This checks the scorecard, not the meeting. Raw agreement flatters: two people who answer Yes to everything would agree 90% of the time without looking. Kappa removes the agreement you would get by luck. 0.75 or higher is good, 0.40 to 0.74 means tighten the disputed statements, below 0.40 is not reliable yet.

Good: 0.75 or higher across at least 5 paired walks.

P-value: how sure can we be?

Is the pattern real, or luck?

Flip a coin 10 times and get 7 heads: that happens by luck all the time, p about 0.34. Get 10 of 10: p about 0.002, something is going on. Below 0.05 is sure enough to call it real. 0.05 to 0.10 is maybe, keep walking. Above 0.10, can't tell yet. With about one walk a day, expect a direction within a month and a firm answer within a quarter.

Why this exists

SignalWalk is a field tool for the person who audits tier meetings: a consultant, an operations excellence lead, or a plant manager walking the floor. It scores a live tier meeting against seven dimensions (eight at Tier 2) that separate a meeting that regulates from one that just occupies a slot on the calendar. Every dimension is scored from things you can see or count in the room, not from an impression.

Purpose

Sit in on any Tier 1, 2, or 3 meeting. Read each statement and answer Yes if you agree, No if you don't, as it happens. Enter the minutes late and the run time, mark who was in the room, and walk out with a scored profile and an IMI read for that tier.

Inputs

Site name, date, tier observed, meeting, chair, shift, observer, minutes late, total run time, a Yes (agree) or No (disagree) on each statement, Present, Delegate, or Absent for each required role, a hesitation count, and the escalation sort when anything was escalated. The evidence note under each dimension is optional, but it is what turns the score into client-ready language. Actual and target numbers on the results are optional, but they strengthen the analysis.

Outputs

A rating for each dimension that the tool works out from your answers, a profile bar chart, an overall IMI maturity band, the list of checks that missed, and a saved history entry. Repeat visits to the same site show the trend and which roles keep missing the meeting. The Dashboard places every meeting you walk on the IMI scale in one view. The Analysis tab turns the history into a sigma level, a control chart, a sustainment read, a Pareto of misses, a before and after test, a regression of results on meeting score, and an observer calibration check. History downloads as a CSV for Excel.

Cadence

Use it every time you observe a tier meeting in the field, live and in the room. Score the same site again on a later visit to see whether the meeting is regulating better or worse than last time.

Escalation path

This tool does not escalate anything itself. A Failure mode on Escalation, Actions, or Attendance is the signal to raise the pattern directly with the chair or the client sponsor before the next scheduled walk.

Supporting documents

Pairs with the Tier Meeting Diagnostic and the Meeting Signal Review on mosei.org. This tool is the one used in the room, by the observer, while the meeting runs.

Three things most likely to fail in year one

Answering the checks from memory after the meeting instead of live, in the room. Not using the timer, so minutes late and run time become guesses. Treating one weak visit as the verdict instead of checking it against the history trend.

How each dimension is rated

Every check is written as a positive statement. Yes means you agree with it. No means you don't. Yes is always the good answer, so every No is a miss.

The tool counts the misses in each dimension: none is Good, one is Drifting, two or more is Failure mode. You never pick the rating yourself.

A few statements are marked Not scored. They only decide which checks apply, such as whether anything was escalated. When a Tier 2 dimension does not apply that day, it is marked N/A and left out of the overall score.

One time rule runs through the whole tool: 1 minute. A meeting starting more than 1 minute late, a run time more than 1 minute over the limit, an off-board topic running past 1 minute, or a deep dive not parked within 1 minute all count against the meeting.

1. Start

Enter the minutes late, then answer: The chair started without waiting for anyone. Within 1 minute is Good. More than 1 and up to 3 minutes is Drifting. More than 3 minutes, or a No on the statement, is Failure mode.

What can go wrong: the meeting waits for one person. A Tier 1 huddle that starts 3 minutes late has lost a fifth of its time, usually the Top 3 issues block.

2. Preparation

  1. The board was updated before the meeting started.
  2. Attendees had their own numbers ready.
  3. Every metric on the board shows target, actual, and red or green status.

What can go wrong: the first several minutes are spent catching people up instead of deciding anything, and a board with no targets or status colors cannot tell anyone what needs attention.

3. Focus

  1. The board was walked in order, Safety first.
  2. Every issue was tied to a number, trend, or red item. No if any issue ran over 1 minute on story or opinion alone.
  3. Every off-board topic was parked with an owner within 1 minute.
  4. The meeting ran from the board, not slides, email, laptops, or phones. At Tier 3 the site dashboard or weekly pack counts as the board.

What can go wrong: the loudest voice sets the agenda regardless of what the board shows.

4. Actions (Tier 1 and Tier 3)

  1. Every new action got a named owner and a due date.
  2. Each action owner was present and agreed out loud.
  3. Actions were written on the board or Action Log during the meeting.
  4. The action log was read. Each item was closed, confirmed against its countermeasure, or recommitted.

If no new actions were raised, statements 1 to 3 are skipped and Actions is rated on the log read alone. Not every problem belongs on the log, so a meeting with nothing new can still be healthy.

What can go wrong: "we should look into that" gets said and the meeting moves on. No owner, no date, and it quietly disappears.

5. Escalation (Tier 1 and Tier 3)

  1. Every skip-the-log item was escalated, not logged. Safety repeats, customer escapes, ship date risk, and anything needing authority or money this room doesn't have.
  2. Every log item on day 3 or later has a confirmed countermeasure (owner, date, recovery plan) or was escalated.
  3. Every escalation was a skip-the-log item or a day 3 miss, not just a hard problem.
  4. Each escalation stated What, Why, and Need.
  5. Every escalation from the tier below was answered within 24 hours. Tier 3 only.

If nothing was escalated, statements 3 and 4 are skipped.

What can go wrong: the chair holds the issue. "We'll handle it ourselves" becomes the answer, and by the time it surfaces it costs more to fix or has already reached the customer.

Tier 2: Action Log, Escalations, A3 Status

At Tier 2, Actions and Escalation are replaced by three short dimensions that match how the meeting runs: an action log under each element on the board for longer-term issues, the day's escalations, and A3 status.

Action Log

  1. Every board element's action log was reviewed in the meeting.
  2. No open item is past due without a new date agreed in the meeting.

Escalations (N/A if none were on the agenda)

  1. Every new escalation was read out and given a named owner in the meeting.
  2. Every escalation from the last meeting was reported closed or given a new date.
  3. Every escalation from the tier below was answered within 24 hours.

A3 Status (N/A if no A3 was presented)

  1. Each A3 owner stated what changed since the last review and asked for a specific decision or help.
  2. Every countermeasure discussed was backed by evidence of the cause shown in the meeting.

What can go wrong: the logs go stale while the meeting looks busy, escalations get an owner and are never asked about again, and A3s become status reports with the fix chosen before the cause is shown.

6. Attendance

Mark each required role Present, Delegate, or Absent. A delegate counts only if they can decide: if they defer anything in the meeting ("I'll have to check"), mark them Absent. All roles covered is Good. One role missing is Drifting. Two or more missing, or the chair absent with no stand-in, is Failure mode.

  • Tier 1: Team Lead (chair), Crew.
  • Tier 2: Supervisor (chair), Safety, Quality, Production, Maintenance, Materials, Engineering, HR.
  • Tier 3: VP Ops (chair), Leadership team.

Role lists can be edited per site with Edit roles. History shows which roles keep missing.

What can go wrong: the meeting runs well with the wrong people. A Tier 2 without Materials cannot clear a material shortage, however good the meeting looks.

7. Energy (last, scored at the close)

  1. Run time, measured. Limit is 15 minutes for Tier 1 and Tier 2, 30 minutes for Tier 3. A miss if more than 1 minute over.
  2. Problem-solving was parked with an owner within 1 minute.

What can go wrong: the meeting slides into problem-solving in the room, runs long, and the team learns the meeting is where work goes to wait.

Results (not scored)

  1. Safety: there was no safety incident since the last meeting.
  2. Quality: quality met or exceeded its target.
  3. Delivery: today's delivery target was met.
  4. Productivity: productivity met or exceeded its target.
  5. Cost: cost met or beat its target.
  6. People: the staffing plan was met.

The six results follow a standard SQDC board plus Productivity and People. Walks scored before October 2026 recorded Output, which now shows as Productivity.

Read each one off the board. No if the target was missed or no target was posted.

Any No opens one follow-up: the root cause for the miss is clear and understood. Yes means it was named in the meeting and backed by evidence. No means the room is still asking why. A miss with a known cause is a problem being worked. A miss with no cause is a problem being absorbed.

Results are recorded beside the score, not inside it. The score measures how well the meeting ran. Results measure what the operation delivered. Keeping them apart lets History show whether better meetings bring better results, instead of a bad day on the line pulling down a well-run meeting.

Result numbers (optional)

Under each result, enter the actual and the target read off the board. Safety takes a count of incidents. Set Higher is better or Lower is better once per meeting, for example scrap is lower is better. The tool remembers it. The Yes or No stays the official result. The numbers feed the actual vs target regression and the CSV.

Decision Boundary (not scored)

  1. Hesitations: count every time someone deferred because they were unsure the decision was theirs. Enter 0 if none. Each one is visible Unclaimed Authority.
  2. Escalation sort, when anything was escalated: how many crossed a boundary (needed a decision above this tier's limits), how many ran out of clock (the window would close before this tier could resolve it), and how many were just hard.

Both are counts, not ratings. They sit beside the score so Analysis can show whether authority is being claimed at this tier over time.

How opportunities and defects are counted

Every scored statement that applied is one opportunity. So are the start time, holding the start, the run time, and each required role. A No, a start more than 1 minute late, a run more than 1 minute over, or an absent role is one defect. Results and Decision Boundary counts are not opportunities. Defects divided by opportunities is the miss rate. Per million, it is DPMO. DPMO converts to a sigma level with the usual 1.5 shift.

Analysis tab

  1. Sigma level: DPMO and sigma level for the walks in view. With no misses yet it shows a floor, not a value.
  2. Control chart: a p-chart of the miss rate per walk with 3-sigma limits. Signals are a walk outside the limits, 8 walks in a row on one side of the center line, or 6 walks in a row moving the same way. Limits are provisional until about 20 walks. Pick one meeting so the chart tracks one process.
  3. Sustainment: the chance the next walk is at standard (Regulating or better), the current run at standard, and whether the miss rate is trending.
  4. Learning curve: the learning rate per doubling of meetings held, and the projected date this meeting reaches its target miss rate.
  5. Pareto: which checks miss most, and how much of the total the top 3 carry.
  6. Before and after: pick a date, such as when a coaching module was delivered. A 2-proportion test on checks and a Mann-Whitney test on walks. Trust a change when both agree.
  7. Results: logistic regression of each result on the walk score, for the same walk and for the next walk of the same meeting. Needs 10 walks. With numbers entered, a linear regression of attainment against score.
  8. Decision Boundary: hesitation totals and trend, and the share of escalations that were just hard.

Read the p-value as the chance of seeing a difference this large if nothing had really changed. Below 0.05 is the usual line. One observer and one walk a day gives direction within a month, not proof.

What can go wrong: mixing meetings in one chart, calling a change after three walks, and treating a correlation between meeting score and results as proof of cause.

Participation (not scored)

Count the people in the room and how many spoke at least once, besides the chair. Count each person once. Optional, but it feeds the Participation card in Analysis. Half the room or more is healthy.

Coaching focus and walk report

Before scoring, write the one thing the chair should work on before the next walk. After scoring, tap Share walk report on the Result tab. It sends a plain-text summary by text, email, or any app on your phone: the band and score, the coaching focus, what missed, what went well, and the results. On a computer it copies to the clipboard.

At the next walk of the same meeting, the Coaching focus card shows last walk's focus and asks whether the chair worked on it. Analysis tracks the follow-through rate, so you can see whether your coaching sticks.

What can go wrong: a report with five things to fix. Pick one. The chair will work on one.

Timer

In the Start card, set the meeting's scheduled start once. SignalWalk remembers it for that meeting. Tap Started now the moment the meeting begins, and minutes late fill in. Tap Ended now in the Energy card when it closes, and run time fills in. You can still type either number by hand.

Edit a saved walk

In History, tap Edit this walk. The walk opens in the Score tab with every answer in place. Fix what was wrong and tap Save changes. The walk keeps its place in your history. Walks scored before a statement was added or retired pick up the current statements when edited, so answer any new ones before saving.

Put it on your home screen

On iPhone, open SignalWalk in Safari, tap Share, then Add to Home Screen. It opens like an app, full screen, one tap from your pocket. On Android, use the browser menu and Add to Home screen.

Dashboard

Every meeting you walk, placed on the IMI scale by its latest walk, lowest score first. Each row shows the latest score, the average of the last 5 walks, how many of those were at standard, and the trend against the 5 walks before. Filter by site and tier. The legend at the bottom explains the score, the bands, and the colors.

Backup and restore

Your walks are saved on this phone, in this browser, and nowhere else. Download a backup from History every week or two and keep it somewhere safe. To move to a new phone or browser, open SignalWalk there, tap Restore from backup, and pick the file. Walks already on the device are kept and never duplicated.

What can go wrong: clearing the browser's history or website data deletes every walk. Back up first.

Learning curve

Wright's Law: every time the number of meetings held doubles, the miss rate falls by a fixed share. A learning rate of 80% means each doubling leaves 80% of the misses. The tool counts meetings held, not walks, because the team learns from every meeting it runs.

  1. Live since: the date the meeting started. Blank means counting starts at your first walk. A meeting that ran long before you arrived needs its real start date.
  2. Meetings per week: 5 for a daily meeting on weekdays, 1 for a weekly one. Tier 1 per shift is meetings per week on the line you walk.
  3. Target miss %: where you want this meeting to land.

Pick one meeting. It needs 10 walks spanning at least two doublings of meetings held. It projects the meetings and the date to target, with a range. It projects nothing when misses are not falling, and it flags a plateau when the last 5 walks all sit above the curve. After a chair change or a new agenda, set Live since to the change date and the curve starts over.

Power curves slow down as they go. When the target sits more than 3 years out at the current pace, the tool says so: more repetitions will not close the gap, a step change in how the meeting is run will.

What can go wrong: a guessed start date, which bends the curve, and treating the projected date as a promise instead of a pace.

Observer calibration

Two observers score the same meeting live, separately. The second observer taps Copy for calibration on that walk in History and sends it. Paste it in Analysis, pair it with your walk, and the tool shows Cohen's Kappa and where you disagreed. Pairs build up, so Kappa improves in accuracy with each paired walk. 0.75 or higher means the statements are clear enough that the score does not depend on who is watching. Below that, rewrite the most disputed statements.

Action Log rules

Not every problem goes on the log. Only a repeat within 30 days, or a miss too big to wait, that this room can fix. The day it is logged is day 1, counting meeting days, not calendar days. An item with a countermeasure confirmed by day 3 (owner, date, recovery plan) is held to its due date and does not escalate.

IMI: the Implementation Maturity Index

The IMI is the seven-level maturity scale used across the MOSei tools. It measures how far a management operating system element, here a tier meeting, has moved from existing on paper to running the business. Each walk's score places the meeting on the scale for that visit.

  1. Dark: not formally in place. The basics have to be established first.
  2. Named: named but not run consistently.
  3. Started: runs some of the time. The gap between existing and practiced is the work.
  4. Compliant: runs to the steps, but does not yet change how people act.
  5. Regulating: runs, is used, and changes behavior. The critical threshold, and SignalWalk's standard.
  6. Embedded: part of how work gets done. It holds without the observer in the room.
  7. Compounding: returns more than its parts. Protect it and extend it under pressure.

Score ranges: Dark 0 to 20%, Named 21 to 35%, Started 36 to 50%, Compliant 51 to 65%, Regulating 66 to 80%, Embedded 81 to 90%, Compounding 91 to 100%. These match the MOS Assessment on mosei.org, so a meeting lands on the same level in every MOSei tool.
